\begin{abstract}
A \ransac score ranks candidate models by how well they explain the
observed geometric errors. A recent likelihood-based score integrated out the unknown
inlier noise level but still required an outlier width: a distance that
specifies how far erroneous observations can lie from the model. We
remove this last distance parameter. For each candidate model, we consider
successively larger sets of inliers and estimate the outlier width from
the largest residual coordinates among the remaining observations.
Extreme-value theory relates the distribution of these large errors to
the upper limit of the outlier distribution. To our knowledge, this is
the first use of an extreme-value model of geometric errors to score
\ransac hypotheses. \evsac used extreme values of descriptor distances to
guide sampling. Our score requires only a dimensionless choice of which
fraction of the upper tail to fit, and changing residual units preserves
the ranking of hypotheses. The fitted tail also defines a nested
extreme-value outlier component, which reduces to the uniform one at a
shape of $-1$. Removing the width exposes the remaining prior choice,
the probability that an observation is an inlier. We show that a
calibrated width largely compensates for fixing this mixing parameter:
in a synthetic validation, marginalizing it returns the calibrated width
to the true outlier support, where the extreme-value estimate matches
calibrated performance. Profiling over hard partitions then collapses
when inlier and outlier residuals overlap heavily; summing the labels
out does not, and with the extreme-value outlier component it gives
the best result when the outliers vary between hypotheses, above the
best calibrated \gaussunif mixture.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A geometric hypothesis can agree closely with many correspondences by
chance. To rank hypotheses, \ransac~\cite{Fischler1981} therefore needs
some account of how much agreement to expect from inliers and from
outliers. A distance threshold answers both questions with one number.
Its value depends on the residual, the coordinate units and the
localization accuracy of the observations.

The profile-marginal score of Pritts et~al.~\cite{Pritts2026} separates
the two questions. For a
given set of \tinliers, it integrates out the unknown scale of
their Gaussian residuals. It assigns the remaining observations a uniform
outlier density and selects the partition with the highest score. This
leaves one parameter in residual units: the half-width $a$ of the uniform
distribution. A large $a$ makes a chance agreement less likely than a
small $a$, so this choice can change which hypothesis wins.

We estimate $a$ from the geometric errors already available when scoring
a hypothesis. Each \tinlier set specifies its own \toutliers. Small residuals in this complement are ambiguous: a short
inlier set can leave many genuine inliers just outside it. Large absolute
residual coordinates, however, carry information about the outer boundary
of the outlier distribution. We fit their upper tail by peaks over
threshold~\cite{Balkema1974,Pickands1975,Coles2001} and use its estimated
endpoint as the half-width in the score. The threshold for fitting the
tail is an empirical quantile. Choosing its level requires no knowledge
of the units or magnitude of the errors.

Removing the width exposes a second prior choice, which has no units:
the probability $\gamma$ that an observation is an inlier. The earlier
score fixes $\gamma=\tfrac12$. We show that, at a fixed width, changing
$\gamma$ is exactly equivalent to rescaling $a$, so a calibrated width
tunes the mixing parameter as much as the outlier support. Integrating
$\gamma$ out under its conjugate prior separates the two, keeps the
search exact and preserves the invariance to units.

The state of the art saturates on the \gaussunif mixture: its scores
differ in how they treat the inlier scale, not in what they assume
about outliers~\cite{Shekhovtsov2025}. We therefore keep the Gaussian
inlier component fixed and ask whether an extreme-value outlier
component can do better. We explore two outlier components and two ways
of eliminating the labels, and select between them on performance.

\paragraph{Extreme values in sampling and scoring.}
Fragoso et al.~\cite{Fragoso2013evsac} introduced extreme-value theory
into \ransac through \evsac. A query descriptor has many incorrect
candidate matches, and nearest-neighbor matching selects the one with
the smallest descriptor distance if it beats the correct match.
\evsac models the minimum among incorrect matches with a generalized
extreme-value distribution, fitted using second-nearest-neighbor
distances. A Gamma model for correct matches completes the mixture.
The resulting correspondence weights guide the sampling of minimal sets
before a geometric hypothesis exists.

Here the random variable, the extreme and the decision all change. We
model large geometric residual coordinates under a particular hypothesis,
fit their tail anew for each candidate partition, and use its endpoint
to rank hypotheses. To our knowledge, this is the first \ransac score to
use an extreme-value model of outlier geometric errors in the hypothesis
likelihood. The sampler and score can be used together, as can the score
and any sampler guided by match quality, such as
\prosac~\cite{ChumMatas2005}.

\paragraph{Geometric scoring.}
Inlier counting and \msac~\cite{Torr1998} require an inlier tolerance.
\mlesac~\cite{Torr2000} scores a Gaussian--uniform mixture at a specified
inlier scale. These choices are related: fixing a Gaussian quantile
converts a scale into a distance tolerance~\cite{Shekhovtsov2025}.
\magsac and \magsacpp~\cite{Barath2019,Barath2020} integrate over a range
of scales whose upper limit must still be specified. Adaptive estimators,
including \assc, \askc, \simfit and \simfitpp
\cite{Wang2004,Wang2010,Heinrich2013,Edstedt2025}, infer a scale from
the residuals. Our score integrates it out separately for each \tinlier set. \recon~\cite{Raguram2011} instead uses agreement between
residual rankings, and learned scores rank hypotheses from data, with
correspondences~\cite{Barath2022} or without them~\cite{BarrosoLaguna2023}.
The mixture itself is the outlier-process view of robust estimation, in
which a robust loss is an inlier density with an outlier
alternative~\cite{BlackRangarajan1996}; \mlesac fits its mixing parameter by
expectation maximization~\cite{Torr2000,Dempster1977}, and Torr treats the
mixture's parameters in a Bayesian way~\cite{Torr2002}. The a-contrario methods \minpran~\cite{Stewart1995}
and \orsa/\acransac~\cite{Moisan2004,Moisan2012,Moulon2012} also avoid prescribing
an inlier scale. They quantify chance agreement under a specified
background model. We estimate the background extent from each
hypothesis's residuals within the Gaussian--uniform scoring model.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item We replace the prescribed outlier half-width by an endpoint
  estimate, prove that under Jeffreys' prior a change of residual units
  preserves both the selected partition and the hypothesis ranking, and
  show why thresholds within a generalized Pareto tail target the same
  endpoint.
 \item We keep the inlier component fixed and propose two outlier
  components: a uniform one whose width is estimated by extreme-value
  theory, and a generalized Pareto distribution on the residual
  coordinates themselves, which reduces to the first at $\xi=-1$. We show
  why the second must be built on coordinates rather than distances.
 \item We eliminate the labels in two ways, by profiling over hard
  partitions with an exact sort-and-sweep and by summing them out, and
  compare both formulations with both outlier components.
 \item We show that a supplied width and a fixed mixing parameter are
  interchangeable, and integrate the mixing parameter out under its
  conjugate Beta prior without losing the exactness of the search.
 \item A synthetic validation tests each claim, including where the
  extreme-value component and the marginalized mixing parameter fail.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The generative model}
\label{sec:method}


\label{sec:model}

Consider $N$ observations $\bx_i \in \Reals^d$, $i = 1,\ldots,N$, and
a candidate geometric model~$\btheta \in \Reals^{n_\theta}$. The
noise-free point~$\bar\bx_i$ of each inlier lies on the model manifold
$\cM_{\btheta} = \{\bx \in \Reals^d : \bg(\bx,\btheta) = \bzero\}$,
whose codimension $d_g = d - \dim\cM_{\btheta}$ is the number of
directions normal to it. Noise along~$\cM_{\btheta}$ leaves~$\bg$
unchanged to first order, so only the $d_g$ normal directions carry
evidence. The codimension therefore fixes the dimension of the
residual vector, and each observation imposes $d_g$ independent scalar
constraints on~$\btheta$, one per normal direction. A minimal sample
has $m = \lceil n_\theta/d_g \rceil$ observations, the fewest whose
constraints determine~$\btheta$ up to finitely many solutions. A
minimal solver computes these solutions in the hypothesis step that
proposes models for scoring.

An inlier is a noisy observation of a point on the model,
$\bx_i = \bar\bx_i + \bvarepsilon_i$ with $\bg(\bar\bx_i,\btheta) = \bzero$
and $\bvarepsilon_i \sim \NN(\bzero, \sigma^2\btSigma_{\bx_i})$, where the
shape $\btSigma_{\bx_i}$ is known and the inlier scale~$\sigma$ is
not. To first order $\bg_i \coloneqq \bg(\bx_i,\btheta) \approx
\bJ_i\bvarepsilon_i$, with $\bJ_i$ the Jacobian of~$\bg$ with respect
to~$\bx$. Whitening by $\bL_i\bL_i^\top = \bJ_i\btSigma_{\bx_i}\bJ_i^\top$
gives the residual
\begin{equation}
 \br_i \coloneqq \bL_i^{-1}\bg_i \;\sim\; \NN(\bzero, \sigma^2\bI_{d_g}),
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
whose squared norm $\delta_i^2 = \|\br_i\|^2 =
\bg_i^\top(\bJ_i\btSigma_{\bx_i}\bJ_i^\top)^{-1}\bg_i$ is the general
form of the squared Sampson
distance~\cite{Sampson1982,Hartley2003,Chojnacki2000}. Equivalently,
it is the squared Mahalanobis distance, with respect
to~$\btSigma_{\bx_i}$, from~$\bx_i$ to the linearization
of~$\cM_{\btheta}$ at~$\bx_i$. Its relation to the geometric distance
to~$\cM_{\btheta}$ is discussed in~\cite{Harker2006,Rydell2024}. The
model requires only that the distribution of the inlier residual be
isotropic normal, and for two-view geometry this is well supported.
Marginalizing the unknown true point along the manifold makes the density
of the residual along any ray normal to it exactly Gaussian when the
observation noise is Gaussian, for any codimension, and close to Gaussian
when it is not~\cite[Sec.~3.2]{Shekhovtsov2025}. Inlier Sampson errors on
PhotoTourism and HPatches agree with this model, with scales that vary
between keypoints~\cite[Fig.~3]{Shekhovtsov2025}, and Edstedt
et~al.~\cite{Edstedt2025} report Gaussian-like residuals for relative
pose across matchers.

We adopt the standard \gaussunif
mixture~\cite{Torr2000,Shekhovtsov2025}: a latent label
$z_i \in \{0,1\}$ marks observation~$i$ as inlier ($z_i{=}1$) or outlier
($z_i{=}0$) with prior $\Pr(z_i{=}1) = \gamma$, and the
residual~$\br_i$ has conditional density
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 p(\br_i \mid z_i{=}1, \btheta, \sigma) &= \pin(\br_i;\sigma)
   \coloneqq (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d_g/2}\,e^{-\|\br_i\|^2/2\sigma^2},\\
 p(\br_i \mid z_i{=}0, \btheta, a) &= p_a(\br_i) \coloneqq (2a)^{-d_g},
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:component-densities}
\end{equation}
a Gaussian inlier density and a uniform outlier density on the cube
$[-a,a]^{d_g}$, zero outside it. A random mismatch has unrelated
components, so its density is uniform over the observation domain, up to
the domain's boundary~\cite[Sec.~3.2.2]{Shekhovtsov2025}; the boundary is
where outlier component~II departs from the uniform one. The half-width~$a$ has the same units
as~$\sigma$ (\eg pixels for a Sampson distance). The earlier
score~\cite{Pritts2026} fixes it by normalizing coordinates so that the
image half-diagonal is one; here it is estimated from the residuals.
The inlier component is fixed (\cref{sec:inlier}); we propose two
outlier components, a uniform one whose width is estimated by
extreme-value theory (\cref{sec:outlier}) and an extreme-value
distribution on the residuals themselves (\cref{sec:gpd-component}).

Collecting the inlier labels defines a \tinlier set
$\cI \coloneqq \{i : z_i = 1\}$, with complement
$\cO = \{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\cI$ of \toutliers, $n_I = |\cI|$ and
$n_O = |\cO|$. By conditional independence, the likelihood of a
partition is
\begin{equation}
 p(\br\mid\btheta,\cI,\sigma,a)
 =\prod_{i\in\cI}\pin(\br_i;\sigma)
  \prod_{i\in\cO}p_a(\br_i).
 \label{eq:partition-likelihood}
\end{equation}
This is the model under which Shekhovtsov~\cite{Shekhovtsov2025} analyzes
\ransac scores: \msac is its profile likelihood over the labels and \mlesac
its marginal likelihood, both at a threshold that is a fixed quantile of the
inlier scale, and when tuned they select models almost identically. The two
ways of eliminating the labels in \cref{sec:inference} are these two, with
the inlier scale integrated out and the outlier support estimated.
As in~\cite{Pritts2026}, $\gamma=\tfrac12$ gives every partition the same
prior weight $2^{-N}$, and we treat the partition as a free variable to
optimize rather than labelling each point at a fixed~$\sigma$;
\cref{sec:label-prior} integrates $\gamma$ out instead.

\subsection{The inlier component}
\label{sec:evpm}
\label{sec:inlier}

The inlier component is the Gaussian of \cref{eq:component-densities}
with its scale integrated out, as in the earlier score~\cite{Pritts2026}.
At a fixed partition the integral has a closed form.

Write $\RSS_\cI=\sum_{i\in\cI}\delta_i^2$ and
$\nu=n_I d_g-n_\theta$. The latter is the residual degrees-of-freedom
adjustment used in the earlier score for a fitted model. With the
inverse-gamma prior $\IG(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$, its adjusted inlier term
is, up to a common additive constant,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 A(\cI)={}&\log\Gamma(\nu/2+\alpha_0)-\tfrac{\nu}{2}\log(2\pi)\\
 &-(\nu/2+\alpha_0)\log(\RSS_\cI/2+\beta_0).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:evpm}
\end{equation}
In the limit of Jeffreys' prior~\cite{Jeffreys1946}, $\alpha_0=\beta_0=0$ and
$\pi(\sigma^2)\propto1/\sigma^2$; the integral requires $\nu>0$
and $\RSS_\cI>0$.

\subsection{Outlier component I: uniform, with an extreme-value width}
\label{sec:outlier}

The uniform outlier model assigns residual vectors to the cube
$[-a,a]^{d_g}$. Its half-width is a coordinate bound. A vector near a
corner can have norm as large as $\sqrt{d_g}\,a$, so fitting the upper
tail of the norms would generally target the wrong endpoint. We instead
pool the absolute coordinates of the \toutliers, retaining
multiplicities:
\begin{equation}
 \cZ(\cO)=\bigl(|r_{ij}|:i\in\cO,\ j=1,\ldots,d_g\bigr).
 \label{eq:pooled}
\end{equation}
Under the uniform model, each absolute coordinate is uniform on $[0,a]$.

Choose a quantile level $\eta$ and set $u=Q_\eta(\cZ(\cO))$.
For $z>u$, the excess is $y=z-u$. Under the usual extreme-value
domain-of-attraction conditions, a generalized Pareto distribution
approximates the excess distribution at a sufficiently high
threshold~\cite{Balkema1974,Pickands1975}. Its distribution function is
\begin{equation}
 G_u(y)=1-(1+\xi y/\omega)^{-1/\xi},
 \label{eq:gpd}
\end{equation}
for $y\geq0$, $1+\xi y/\omega>0$ and $\omega>0$, with the
exponential limit at $\xi=0$. A negative shape $\xi$ gives the finite
endpoint $u-\omega/\xi$. For a uniform coordinate the excess is
exactly uniform on $[0,a-u]$, so $\xi=-1$ and $\omega=a-u$.

We fit shape and scale by probability-weighted
moments~\cite{Hosking1987}. For a finite-endpoint fit, the estimated
half-width and corresponding uniform density level are
\begin{align}
 \ahat(\cO)&=u-\hat\omega/\hat\xi,  \label{eq:endpoint}\\
 \pout(\cO)&=(2\ahat(\cO))^{-d_g}.     \label{eq:pout}
\end{align}
In the score of this subsection the generalized Pareto law supplies only
the endpoint; the density used to score an outlier remains uniform.
\Cref{sec:gpd-component} uses the fitted shape as well.

\paragraph{Rules.}
The threshold $u$ is the empirical $\eta$-quantile with linear
interpolation. With fewer than five excesses, or a fitted shape
$\hat\xi\geq-10^{-3}$, which has no finite endpoint, $\ahat$ falls back
to the largest coordinate in the complement. A fitted endpoint below
the largest coordinate is raised to it, so that every \toutliers lie in
the support and the uniform likelihood is finite. Each rule is
equivariant under a change of units.

\subsection{Outlier component II: an extreme-value distribution on the residuals}
\label{sec:gpd-component}

Outlier component I uses the generalized Pareto fit only for its
endpoint. The fit also estimates a shape. By threshold
stability (\cref{sec:properties}), a generalized Pareto law above $u$ is
the restriction of the same law above any lower threshold. Carrying the
fitted law down to zero gives the density of an absolute outlier
coordinate,
\begin{equation}
 f(z;\hat\xi,\ahat)=\frac{1}{\omega_0}
 \Bigl(1+\frac{\hat\xi z}{\omega_0}\Bigr)^{-1/\hat\xi-1},
 \quad 0\leq z<\ahat,\quad \omega_0=|\hat\xi|\,\ahat,
 \label{eq:gpd-density}
\end{equation}
and, with independent coordinates and symmetric signs, the outlier
density $p(\br_i)=\prod_j\tfrac12 f(|r_{ij}|)$, which replaces $p_a$ in
\cref{eq:partition-likelihood}. At $\hat\xi=-1$, $f=1/\ahat$ and this
is the uniform component: the \gaussunif score is the special case
$\xi=-1$ of a Gaussian--generalized-Pareto mixture, so the two models are
nested. For $\hat\xi>-1$ the density falls toward the endpoint, and its
value at the model, $f(0)=1/(|\hat\xi|\ahat)$, exceeds the uniform
level. Extending the tail law down to zero is an assumption, and it is
the assumption the plug-in score avoids: tail shape alone does not
identify the density near zero.

\paragraph{Coordinates, not distances.}
For $d_g=1$ the absolute coordinate is the distance. For $d_g\geq2$ the
two differ. A density $f(\delta)$ on distances defines the residual
density $f(\delta)/(S_{d_g-1}\delta^{d_g-1})$, where $S_{d_g-1}$ is the
area of the unit sphere. If $f(0)>0$, as for a generalized Pareto law
carried down to zero, this residual density diverges at the model for
$d_g\geq2$, and the outlier component would claim the inliers. The norm
of a uniform vector in the cube also thins out linearly toward the
corners ($\xi=-\tfrac12$ for $d_g=2$), so a model on distances would
read the geometry of the cube as thinning outliers. On coordinates the
uniform cube has $\xi=-1$ exactly. This is the distinction Shekhovtsov
draws between the density of the residual along a ray normal to the
manifold, which defines the likelihood, and the distribution of the
residual's norm, which does not~\cite[Sec.~5.1]{Shekhovtsov2025}.

\paragraph{Endpoint.}
For $\hat\xi>-1$ the density vanishes at the endpoint, so the largest
coordinate cannot lie on it. Where the fitted endpoint does not exceed
the largest coordinate $z_{(n)}$, the component uses
$z_{(n)}+(z_{(n)}-z_{(n-1)})$, which is also equivariant under a change
of units.

\subsection{The mixing parameter}
\label{sec:label-prior}

With a general prior $\gamma$, the label prior adds
$n_I\log\gamma+n_O\log(1-\gamma)$ to the score. At a fixed width,
changing $\gamma$ to $\gamma'$ changes every partition's score by
$n_I\log\frac{\gamma'(1-\gamma)}{\gamma(1-\gamma')}$ plus a constant,
and changing $a$ to $a'$ changes it by $n_I d_g\log(a'/a)$ plus a
constant. The two are therefore interchangeable: the scores at
$(\gamma',a)$ and at $(\gamma,a')$ select the same partitions when
\begin{equation}
 a'=a\left(\frac{\gamma'/(1-\gamma')}{\gamma/(1-\gamma)}\right)^{1/d_g}.
 \label{eq:gamma-width}
\end{equation}
With $\gamma=\tfrac12$ fixed, a width calibrated on data therefore
absorbs any mismatch between $\tfrac12$ and the actual inlier fraction,
and tunes the label prior as much as the outlier support.

The earlier score noted that $\gamma$ can be integrated out under its
conjugate prior~\cite{Pritts2026}. Under $\gamma\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_\gamma,\beta_\gamma)$,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^1\gamma^{n_I}(1-\gamma)^{n_O}\,\pi(\gamma)\,d\gamma
 =\frac{B(n_I+\alpha_\gamma,\,n_O+\beta_\gamma)}{B(\alpha_\gamma,\beta_\gamma)},
 \label{eq:label-marginal}
\end{equation}
and the score gains the logarithm of this term. It depends on the
partition only through $n_I$, so the best partition of each size, and
with it the exactness of the search in \cref{sec:computation}, is
unchanged; it is dimensionless, so \cref{prop:invariance} still holds.
Profiling $\gamma$ at $n_I/N$ instead differs from it by $O(\log N)$.
Marginalizing $\gamma$ separates the two roles of the width: $\ahat$
estimates the support, and the partition determines the inlier fraction.
We use Jeffreys' prior, $\alpha_\gamma=\beta_\gamma=\tfrac12$.

\section{Eliminating the labels}
\label{sec:inference}

\paragraph{Names.}
Each score is named for its mixture. The inlier component is always the
Gaussian with its scale integrated out, written Stu-$t$; the profile-marginal
score with a supplied width is \stuU. With outlier component~I it is
\stuUev, with component~II \stuGP; integrating the mixing parameter out
appends $\beta$ (\stuUevB, \stuGPB), and summing the labels out prefixes
soft- (\soft{\stuGPB}).

Both outlier components, the inlier component and the mixing parameter
define one generative model. Scoring a hypothesis requires eliminating
its labels, and we compare two ways of doing so: profiling over hard
partitions, which the sort-and-sweep makes exact and fast, and summing
each label out, which keeps every residual's label soft.

\subsection{Hard labels: profiling over partitions}
\label{sec:computation}

With the uniform outlier component of \cref{sec:outlier}, the score of
a partition is
\begin{equation}
 S(\btheta,\cI)=A(\cI)-n_O d_g\log\bigl(2\ahat(\cO)\bigr).
 \label{eq:evpm-closed}
\end{equation}
This expression uses the uniform density level in \cref{eq:pout};
its interpretation as a log-likelihood assumes support coverage.
Supplying a fixed $a$ recovers the earlier score. With the
generalized Pareto component, the outlier term is
$\sum_{i\in\cO}\log p(\br_i)$ from \cref{sec:gpd-component}, and with
$\gamma$ integrated out the score gains the logarithm of
\cref{eq:label-marginal}.

Sort the observations by $\delta_i$. For each admissible prefix
$\cI_k=\{(1),\ldots,(k)\}$, accumulate its sum of squares, fit the
upper tail of its complement, and evaluate \cref{eq:evpm-closed}.
The hypothesis score is
\begin{equation}
 S^*(\btheta)=\max_{k}S(\btheta,\cI_k).
 \label{eq:evpm-profile}
\end{equation}
Thus we marginalize the inlier scale and profile over the prefix length;
we call $S^*$ the \emph{extreme-value profile-marginal score}. The
search covers all partitions induced by a threshold on residual norm.
For fixed $a$, the best partition of each size is a prefix because
exchanging an inlier for a closer observation reduces $\RSS_\cI$.
With a fitted width, the exchange can also change $\ahat$. The earlier
argument therefore gives no guarantee of optimality over arbitrary
subsets.

\Cref{sec:complexity} gives the cost of the sweep with each outlier
component.

\subsection{Soft labels: summing them out}
\label{sec:soft}

Profiling over partitions makes every label hard. The alternative is to
sum each label out, as \mlesac does at a fixed
scale~\cite{Torr2000}, there with the mixing parameter fitted by
expectation maximization~\cite{Dempster1977}. The marginal likelihood of a hypothesis is then
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 p(\br\mid\btheta)={}&\iint\prod_{i=1}^{N}
 \bigl[\gamma\,\pin(\br_i;\sigma)+(1-\gamma)\,p(\br_i)\bigr]\\
 &\times\pi(\sigma^2)\,\pi(\gamma)\,d\sigma^2\,d\gamma,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:soft}
\end{equation}
with the outlier density $p(\br_i)$ of either component. The scale is
shared by all residuals, so the product does not factor over $\sigma$
and the integral has no closed form. We evaluate it on a grid of
$\log\sigma$, uniform under Jeffreys' prior and placed relative to the
median residual so that it moves with the units, and on Beta-weighted
cells of $\gamma$. Without a partition there is no complement to fit,
so the outlier component is fitted once per hypothesis, to the upper
tail of all residual coordinates; this assumes that the largest fraction
$1-\eta$ of them are outliers. Where labels are needed, each is the
posterior inlier probability averaged over the grid, thresholded at
$\tfrac12$. Every ingredient is equivariant under a change of units,
so \cref{prop:invariance} holds for this score as well;
\cref{sec:complexity} gives its cost.

\subsection{Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

\Cref{tab:complexity} lists the cost of scoring one hypothesis with
$N$ observations. The profile-marginal score with a supplied width
costs one sort of the distances and one sweep~\cite{Pritts2026}. With
the inlier term a running sum of squares, the sweep is $\Theta(N)$, and
the sort sets the cost: a least-significant-digit radix sort on the
32-bit keys of the residuals, a fixed number of passes, makes it
$\Theta(N)$ as well. The inlier component and the label prior keep that
cost in every formulation here: the label term depends only on $n_I$.
What changes is the outlier term.

\paragraph{Hard labels, component I.}
For $d_g=1$ the complement of the prefix $\cI_k$ is the suffix
$z_{(k+1)},\ldots,z_{(N)}$ of the sorted distances, and its upper tail is
a suffix of that suffix. The threshold $u$ is an order statistic, or the
interpolation of two, at a known rank, and the first value above it is
found by bisection. The probability-weighted-moment fit needs only the
number $m$ of excesses, their sum, and the rank-weighted sum
$\sum_j j\,y_{(j)}$, and with the suffix sums
$T_1(g)=\sum_{l\geq g}z_{(l)}$ and $T_2(g)=\sum_{l\geq g}(N-l+1)\,z_{(l)}$,
which rank from the top so that a short tail's moments never come from a
difference of two large sums,
\begin{equation}
 \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m} j\,z_{(N-m+j)} = (m+1)\,T_1(g)-T_2(g),
 \qquad g=N-m+1,
 \label{eq:pwm-prefix}
\end{equation}
with the shift by $u$ applied in closed form. Each prefix therefore costs
$O(\log N)$, and the sweep $O(N\log N)$ after the $\Theta(N)$ sort. For
$d_g\geq2$ the pooled coordinates need their own sort, and removing the
next vector deletes $d_g$ coordinates from arbitrary ranks. A segment
tree over the sorted coordinates whose nodes hold a count, a sum and a
rank-weighted sum, merged as
$R=R_{\mathrm{left}}+R_{\mathrm{right}}+n_{\mathrm{left}}\,s_{\mathrm{right}}$,
deletes a coordinate and returns the moments of the top $m$ in
$O(\log(Nd_g))$.

\paragraph{Hard labels, component II.}
The outlier term $\sum_{i\in\cO}\log p(\br_i)$ depends on the shape and
endpoint fitted to that complement, which change with every prefix, and
$\log(1-z/\ahat)$ does not decompose into prefix sums. An exact sweep
costs $\Theta(N^2d_g)$. Fitting the tail once per hypothesis, to the
upper tail of all residual coordinates, makes $\log p(\br_i)$ a fixed
per-observation term and restores a $\Theta(Nd_g)$ sweep. The shortcut
gives up the guarantee that the best partition of each size is a
prefix: with a density that decreases away from the model, moving a
closer observation into the complement raises its outlier density.

\paragraph{Soft labels.}
The tail is fitted once per hypothesis, in $\Theta(Nd_g)$ by selection
and a radix sort of the excesses, and no sort of the distances is
needed. The integral over $J$ cells of $\log\sigma$ and $M$ cells of
$\gamma$ costs $\Theta(NJM)$, a factor $JM$ above the sweep. The cells
are independent, so the grid parallelizes directly, and the posterior of
$\log\sigma$ narrows as $N^{-1/2}$, so a grid centred on its mode can
replace a fixed one.

\paragraph{Measured cost.}
\Cref{tab:timing} times every score on the protocol of~\cite{Pritts2026}:
one thread, the stored hypotheses of \TimingPairs{} PhotoTourism pairs in
two density regimes, each score through the same pool-selection call. Per
hypothesis, \stuUev costs \scoretiming{o1gamma12}{typical}{ratio} times
\stuU at $\bar n=\scoretiming{regime}{typical}{navg}$ points, and
\stuGPB fitted once per hypothesis \scoretiming{o2perhypothesisgammamarg}{typical}{ratio}
times. Refitting the generalized Pareto law for every prefix costs
\scoretiming{o2perprefixgammamarg}{typical}{ratio} times (timed on
\TimingReferencePairs{} pairs per regime); it is the exact reference for the
per-hypothesis form, not a deployable score. The soft-label integral is not

\subsection{Properties}
\label{sec:properties}

\begin{proposition}[Invariance to residual units]
\label{prop:invariance}
Under Jeffreys' prior, multiplying all residuals by $c>0$ preserves the
maximizing prefixes and the ranking of hypotheses of the same model class,
provided admissibility is unchanged by a change of units.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Residual order is unchanged and $\RSS_\cI$ scales by $c^2$.
The quantile, excesses and fitted scale all scale by $c$, while the
fitted shape is dimensionless. Hence $\ahat$ scales by $c$. The total
change in \cref{eq:evpm-closed} is
$-(\nu+n_Od_g)\log c=-(Nd_g-n_\theta)\log c$, common to every
prefix and hypothesis.
\end{proof}
The same argument covers \cref{sec:gpd-component,sec:label-prior}: the
generalized Pareto density of \cref{eq:gpd-density} scales by $1/c$,
exactly like the uniform level, and the label term has no units.
With an informative prior, $\beta_0$ must also be expressed in the new
squared-distance units. The earlier score likewise requires conversion
of its supplied width $a$; here the endpoint fit performs that
conversion automatically.

\paragraph{Quantile stability.}
If excesses above $u_0$ follow a generalized Pareto law with parameters
$(\xi,\omega_0)$, raising the threshold to $u$ within its support gives
the same shape and scale $\omega_0+\xi(u-u_0)$~\cite{Coles2001}. For
$\xi<0$, the new endpoint is still $u_0-\omega_0/\xi$. Thus $\eta$
changes how much data enter the fit while preserving the population
endpoint under the tail model. Finite-sample estimates can change:
a higher threshold leaves fewer excesses, and a lower one may extend
below the range where the approximation holds.

\paragraph{Interpreting a selected partition.}
At fixed width and scale, equating Gaussian and uniform densities gives
the equal-density radius, when the logarithm is nonnegative,
\begin{equation}
 \tau=\hat s\sqrt{2d_g\log\!\left(\frac{\ahat}{\hat s}
                         \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\right)},
 \label{eq:implicit-threshold}
\end{equation}
where $\hat s^2=(\RSS_\cI+2\beta_0)/(\nu+2\alpha_0)$.
This gives a reference radius for the fitted components. The discrete
prefix decision in \cref{eq:evpm-profile} also accounts for the change
in the fitted width between consecutive prefixes.

\paragraph{Which extremes: maxima, not minima.}
\label{sec:why-maxima}
The half-width $a$ is the upper endpoint of the outlier coordinates, so
the upper tail estimates it directly. The minima are nevertheless
tempting. The inlier/outlier decision is made near the model, where the
relevant quantity is the outlier density close to zero, and the minima
measure exactly that. For uniform outliers they also determine $a$:
the minimum of a block of $b$ coordinates has expectation $a/(b+1)$, so
$(b+1)$ times the mean of block minima is an unbiased estimate of $a$.

This argument assumes that the complement contains only outliers. Inside
the sweep it does not. A short prefix leaves most of the genuine inliers
in its complement, and their small residuals become the block minima.
The estimate $\ahat$ then collapses, and the outlier term
$-n_Od_g\log(2\ahat)$ in \cref{eq:evpm-closed} rewards labelling nearly
every observation an outlier. The estimate adapts to the observations
it is meant to classify: each short prefix makes its own complement look
dense, which in turn favors the short prefix. A margin beyond the
partition boundary does not repair this, because the scale estimate
$\hat s$ of a short prefix, and with it any margin proportional to
$\hat s$, is itself small. Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} confirms the
failure.

The largest coordinates behave differently. They come from gross
errors far from the model, remain in the complement for every prefix
that excludes them, and do not change as the sweep passes through the
inliers. The upper-tail estimate therefore cannot adapt to the
partition it scores. The price is that it measures the far edge of the
outlier distribution and assumes the density stays at the same level
down to the model. The fitted shape tests this assumption: a uniform
density has $\xi=-1$, whereas a density decreasing linearly to zero at a
finite endpoint has $\xi=-1/2$. In the latter case the true density
near zero is twice the uniform level $(2\ahat)^{-d_g}$, an error the
score sees only through $\log 2$ per outlier coordinate. Tail shape
alone does not identify the density near zero.

\paragraph{Block maxima or peaks over threshold.}
Extreme-value theory offers two estimators of the upper
endpoint~\cite{Coles2001}. Block maxima fit a generalized extreme-value
law to the largest value of each of several blocks. Peaks over
threshold fit the generalized Pareto law of \cref{eq:gpd} to every value
above a threshold. Both target the same endpoint. Peaks over threshold
uses all large values rather than one per block, so it uses the tail
more efficiently, and its one setting, the level $\eta$, is
dimensionless; a block count also has no units, but splitting the
complement into random blocks adds sampling variability. We use peaks
over threshold and report the mean of block maxima as a baseline.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Synthetic validation}
\label{sec:synthetic}

\paragraph{Generative model.}
The experiment validates the score itself, not a geometric pipeline. It
fixes one hypothesis, so there is no model to fit ($n_\theta=0$,
$\nu=n_I$) and no ranking of competing hypotheses, and it uses scalar
residuals ($d_g=1$). Each trial draws $N=\syncase{uniform}{N}$ residual distances: the
inliers from $|\NN(0,1)|$, the outliers on $[0,A]$. The outliers vary over
\SynNumCases{} settings: uniform with \syncase{uniform}{nin} inliers and $A=\syncase{uniform}{halfwidth}$; uniform with \syncase{uniform_3pct}{nin}
inliers ($\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$); uniform with $A=\syncase{uniform_A10}{halfwidth}$, where the two populations overlap
heavily; a density decreasing linearly to zero at $A=\syncase{linear}{halfwidth}$; and a mixed
setting in which each trial draws its own shape, uniform or linearly
decaying, and its own extent, log-uniform on $[\syncase{mixed}{halfwidthmin},\syncase{mixed}{halfwidthmax}]$. Every method
scores the same draws, \SynTrials{} trials per setting, with Jeffreys'
prior on $\sigma^2$. We report the selected prefix length $k^*$ and the
F1 score of the selected partition against the true labels.

\paragraph{Methods.}
\emph{Known $a$} supplies the true extent $A$ of each trial.
\emph{Calibrated $a$} is the best of \SynGridN{} log-spaced widths
between 1 and 3000 by mean F1 over the very trials it is scored on; it
is an in-sample optimum, so it bounds any cross-validated width from
above and favors the baseline. The estimates from each prefix's
complement are the mean of 20 block maxima or of 20 block minima, and
peaks over threshold at $\eta\in\{0.8,0.9,0.95\}$. Each estimate is
scored with outlier component I (\stuUev) and, for peaks over threshold,
with outlier component II (\stuGP), under
hard labels (\cref{sec:computation}) and under soft labels
(\cref{sec:soft}, with the outlier component fitted to the upper tail of
all residuals). Each is scored with the label prior fixed at
$\gamma=\tfrac12$ and with $\gamma$ marginalized
(\cref{sec:label-prior}). \Cref{tab:synthetic} reports $\eta=0.9$.

\paragraph{The endpoint estimate recovers the support.}
With $\gamma=\tfrac12$, the extreme-value width performs as the known
width does in every setting: in the uniform setting both select
\syn{uniform}{half}{pot90-uniform}{k} observations on average, and
across the \SynNumCases{} settings their F1 scores differ by at most
\SynMaxGapKnownEV. Moving $\eta$ from 0.8 to 0.95 changes the F1 score by at
most \SynMaxGapEta. The
median fitted shapes are \syn{uniform}{half}{pot90-uniform}{shape} for
uniform and \syn{linear}{half}{pot90-uniform}{shape} for linearly decaying
outliers, against the population values $-1$ and $-\tfrac12$. The block
minima collapse in every setting to
$k^*\approx\syn{uniform}{half}{block_min20-uniform}{k}$, as predicted in
\cref{sec:why-maxima}. Multiplying every residual by \SynInvScale{}
preserved the selected partition in \SynInvIdentical{} of
\SynInvTrials{} trials, for both components and every label prior.

\paragraph{The true width is not the best width.}
In every setting with a single extent, the calibrated width lies below
the true extent and selects better partitions: in the uniform setting the best width is
\syn{uniform}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{halfwidth} for $A=\syncase{uniform}{halfwidth}$. The failure is
starkest at $\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$ inliers, where the known width selects
\syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{fixed-uniform}{k} observations for \syncase{uniform_3pct}{nin} true
inliers (F1 \syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{fixed-uniform}{f1}) and only the
calibrated width, \syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{halfwidth},
recovers a useful partition (F1
\syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{f1}). \Cref{eq:gamma-width}
explains the gap. With $\gamma=\tfrac12$ and an inlier fraction of
\syncase{uniform}{fraction}, the equivalent width is about
\syncase{uniform}{equivwidth}, close to the calibrated
\syn{uniform}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{halfwidth}; for linearly decaying
outliers, whose density near the model is that of a uniform of
half-width $A/2$, it is about \syncase{linear}{equivwidth}, against
\syn{linear}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{halfwidth}. Calibrating the width mostly
calibrates the mixing parameter.

\paragraph{Marginalizing the mixing parameter.}
If the calibrated width compensates for $\gamma$, then integrating
$\gamma$ out should return the calibrated width to the support and close
the gap. It does in the uniform setting: the calibrated width becomes
\syn{uniform}{marginal}{calibrated-uniform}{halfwidth} for $A=\syncase{uniform}{halfwidth}$, and the
extreme-value width reaches F1
\syn{uniform}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{f1} against the calibrated
\syn{uniform}{marginal}{calibrated-uniform}{f1}. For linearly decaying
outliers the calibrated width moves to
\syn{linear}{marginal}{calibrated-uniform}{halfwidth}, the uniform level that
matches the outlier density at the model, $2/A$. At $\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$ inliers the F1
score of the extreme-value width rises from
\syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1} to
\syn{uniform_3pct}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{f1}, but the partition now
undershoots, at \syn{uniform_3pct}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{k}
observations for \syncase{uniform_3pct}{nin} inliers. Marginalizing fails when the populations
overlap heavily: at $A=\syncase{uniform_A10}{halfwidth}$ the F1 score falls from
\syn{uniform_A10}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1} to
\syn{uniform_A10}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{f1}, and the standard
deviation of $k^*$ grows to
\syn{uniform_A10}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{ksd}. With $\gamma$ free, a
narrow Gaussian core plus outliers can explain part of a dense cloud;
the fixed $\gamma=\tfrac12$ had acted as a regularizer against that.
The mixed setting, which contains dense trials, also loses (F1
\syn{mixed}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1} to
\syn{mixed}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{f1}). Under hard labels,
profiling $\gamma$ selected the same partition as marginalizing it in
\SynProfileSame{} of \SynProfilePairs{} paired cases.

\paragraph{The extreme-value outlier component under hard labels.}
The generalized Pareto component helps where the outliers thin out: for
linearly decaying outliers its F1 score is
\syn{linear}{half}{pot90-gpd}{f1}, against
\syn{linear}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1} for the uniform component. It
ties in the uniform setting (\syn{uniform}{half}{pot90-gpd}{f1} against
\syn{uniform}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1}) and loses where its extra
parameter costs variance: at $\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$ inliers and at $A=\syncase{uniform_A10}{halfwidth}$ the standard
deviation of $k^*$ grows \SynSdRatioMin- to \SynSdRatioMax-fold. In the settings with a single extent it does not reach the calibrated
width. In the mixed setting, every per-trial
estimate (block maxima \syn{mixed}{half}{block_max20-uniform}{f1}, \stuUev
\syn{mixed}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1}, \stuGP
\syn{mixed}{half}{pot90-gpd}{f1}) edges out the single in-sample
calibrated width (\syn{mixed}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{f1}): adapting
to each trial helps, but only slightly.

\paragraph{Soft labels.}
Summing the labels out removes the collapse. With $\gamma$
marginalized and outlier component I, the soft formulation matches the
in-sample calibrated width in the uniform setting (F1
\syn{uniform}{marginal}{soft90-uniform}{f1} against
\syn{uniform}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{f1}), comes close to it at
$\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$ inliers (\syn{uniform_3pct}{marginal}{soft90-uniform}{f1}
against \syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{f1}, where hard
labels reach \syn{uniform_3pct}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{f1}) and at
$A=\syncase{uniform_A10}{halfwidth}$ (\syn{uniform_A10}{marginal}{soft90-uniform}{f1} against
\syn{uniform_A10}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{f1}, where hard labels fall
to \syn{uniform_A10}{marginal}{pot90-uniform}{f1}), with no width
supplied and no setting tuned. A narrow inlier core can no longer claim
part of a dense cloud, because every residual keeps a posterior
probability of either label. Marginalizing $\gamma$ is essential here:
with $\gamma=\tfrac12$ the soft formulation selects
\syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{soft90-uniform}{k} observations at $\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$
inliers (F1 \syn{uniform_3pct}{half}{soft90-uniform}{f1}).

With outlier component II, the soft formulation gives the best result
in the mixed setting, above the \gaussunif mixture at its in-sample
best width. Its F1 score there is \syn{mixed}{marginal}{soft90-gpd}{f1},
against \syn{mixed}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{f1} for the calibrated
width and \syn{mixed}{marginal}{soft90-uniform}{f1} for the uniform
component under the same inference, and the standard deviation of
$k^*$ falls from \syn{mixed}{marginal}{soft90-uniform}{ksd} to
\syn{mixed}{marginal}{soft90-gpd}{ksd}. For linearly decaying outliers
it reaches \syn{linear}{marginal}{soft90-gpd}{f1}, against the
calibrated \syn{linear}{half}{calibrated-uniform}{f1}. Its extra
parameter still costs variance where inliers are few or overlap
heavily (F1 \syn{uniform_3pct}{marginal}{soft90-gpd}{f1} at $\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$
inliers, \syn{uniform_A10}{marginal}{soft90-gpd}{f1} at $A=\syncase{uniform_A10}{halfwidth}$).

\paragraph{Selecting a formulation.}
With $\gamma$ integrated out, soft labels match or beat hard labels in
every setting and with both outlier components. Against hard labels at
$\gamma=\tfrac12$ they lose only at $A=\syncase{uniform_A10}{halfwidth}$
(\stuUevB{} \syn{uniform_A10}{marginal}{soft90-uniform}{f1} against
\syn{uniform_A10}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1}, \stuGPB{}
\syn{uniform_A10}{marginal}{soft90-gpd}{f1} against
\syn{uniform_A10}{half}{pot90-gpd}{f1}) and with component~I in the mixed
setting (\syn{mixed}{marginal}{soft90-uniform}{f1} against
\syn{mixed}{half}{pot90-uniform}{f1}), and they avoid that prior's failure
at $\syncase{uniform_3pct}{percent}\%$ inliers. Soft labels with $\gamma$ marginalized are
the formulation we carry to real data. Between the
outlier components the synthetic experiment does not decide: component
I is the robust choice when inliers are few or overlap heavily,
component II the better one when the outlier density thins out or
varies between hypotheses. Real correspondences make the second case
the common one, so the choice is left to the real pools.

\paragraph{What the experiment does not test.}
It holds the hypothesis fixed and uses scalar residuals with no fitted
parameters. Ranking competing hypotheses, the cost of fitting
$\btheta$, codimension $d_g=2$, and structured outliers are left to the
real pools of \cref{sec:real}.

\subsection{Real-data evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:real}

The synthetic experiment holds the hypothesis fixed. The real-data
question is whether estimating the outlier width changes the ranking
of competing hypotheses. The planned comparison uses the stored
hypothesis pools of~\cite{Pritts2026}: essential matrices on
\phototourism~\cite{Jin2021}, scored by the tangent Sampson
error~\cite{Terekhov2023}, fundamental matrices on \ethd~\cite{Schoeps2017},
and homographies on \heb~\cite{Barath2023}. Scoring the same hypotheses
with every method isolates model selection from sampling and from local
optimization~\cite{Chum2003,Lebeda2012}, which a complete
pipeline~\cite{Barath2025superansac} adds.

The primary comparison keeps the Jeffreys inlier term fixed and varies
only the outlier width: a cross-validated width, a width determined by
image extent, or the fitted endpoint with one quantile level across all
datasets. A further ablation uses the largest absolute coordinate in the complement,
the maximum-likelihood half-width for the uniform cube at a fixed
partition. It also eliminates the supplied width and isolates the
contribution of fitting the upper tail. The external baselines are \acransac, \magsacpp{} and
\simfitpp. The evaluation will report geometric error of the selected
hypothesis, sensitivity to $\eta$ and $a$, and time per hypothesis
including the tail fit. \heb provides a separate test of the uniform
background assumption because correspondences on a competing plane
can form structured outliers. Reporting the fitted shapes alongside
endpoint-fit failures will distinguish departures from a uniform edge
from cases in which the fit yields no usable finite width.
